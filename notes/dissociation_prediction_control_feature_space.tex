\begin{abstract}
We study a noisy linear teacher and a ridge-regression student.  The student
may be trained after a linear feature map, but accentuation is performed by
back-propagating its gradient to the input pixels.  The feature map creates two
distinct geometries: the feature covariance
$C=A\Sigma A^{\top}$ controls natural-image prediction, whereas
$G=AA^{\top}$ controls pixel-space gradient motion.  This distinction gives
closed-form finite-sample expressions for generalization, own-path
accentuation, and peer review.  In a spectral feature map aligned with the
eigenvectors of $\Sigma$, random-matrix deterministic equivalents (DEs) give
per-mode weight bias and variance, generalization error, accentuation error,
slopes, and both $R^2$ measures.  The same DE risk can also approximate
cross-validation and select the ridge penalty.  Full PCA, whitening, and
top-PC truncation emerge as transparent special cases.
\end{abstract}

\section{Teacher, features, and ridge student}

Let the centered input and noisy response be
\begin{equation}
  \bm x\sim\mathcal N(0,\Sigma),\qquad
  y=(\bstar)^{\top}\bm x+\sigma\epsilon,
  \qquad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,1).
\end{equation}
The noiseless teacher signal variance is
\begin{equation}
  S=(\bstar)^{\top}\Sigma\bstar.
\end{equation}
We first map the input to $p$ linear features,
\begin{equation}
  \bm z=A\bm x\in\R^p,
\end{equation}
and fit ridge regression in feature coordinates.  With the training feature
matrix $Z\in\R^{n\times p}$ and
$\widehat C=Z^{\top}Z/n$, the normalized-loss convention is
\begin{equation}
  \that_\lambda
  =\arg\min_{\bm\theta}
   \left\{\frac{1}{n}\lVert\bm y-Z\bm\theta\rVert^2
                 +\lambda\lVert\bm\theta\rVert^2\right\}
  =(\widehat C+\lambda I)^{-1}\frac{Z^{\top}\bm y}{n}.
  \label{eq:feature-ridge}
\end{equation}
The resulting model as a function of pixels is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat f(\bm x)=\that^{\top}A\bm x=\bhat^{\top}\bm x,
  \qquad \bhat=A^{\top}\that.
  \label{eq:pixel-weight}
\end{equation}
Thus feature-space estimation and pixel-space attribution are not the same
object.

\subsection{Best population predictor in the feature span}

Define
\begin{equation}
  C=A\Sigma A^{\top},\qquad
  \bm h=A\Sigma\bstar.
\end{equation}
The best linear feature coefficient and its unrepresented residual are
\begin{align}
  \tstar &= C^{\dagger}\bm h,\\
  r(\bm x)&=(\bstar)^{\top}\bm x-(\tstar)^{\top}A\bm x.
\end{align}
By the normal equations, $\Cov(\bm z,r)=0$.  Since the joint distribution is
Gaussian, the residual is independent of the retained features.  Its variance
is
\begin{equation}
  S_{\perp}=\Var(r)=S-(\tstar)^{\top}C\tstar.
  \label{eq:residual-signal}
\end{equation}
Consequently, truncated features reduce the training model to
\begin{equation}
  y=(\tstar)^{\top}\bm z+\xi,\qquad
  \Var(\xi)=\sigma_{\rm eff}^2=\sigma^2+S_{\perp}.
  \label{eq:effective-noise}
\end{equation}
The omitted teacher signal is simultaneously irreducible generalization error
and additional independent training noise.

\section{Two geometries: prediction versus pixel control}

Natural-image prediction is governed by $C=A\Sigma A^{\top}$.  Pixel-space
gradient motion is governed by the distinct matrix
\begin{equation}
  G=AA^{\top},\qquad \bm q=A\bstar.
  \label{eq:control-geometry}
\end{equation}
The vector $\bm h=A\Sigma\bstar$ determines population prediction, whereas
$\bm q=A\bstar$ determines the teacher's response to a back-propagated pixel
gradient.  Confusing these two vectors hides the prediction--control
dissociation.

\subsection{Natural-distribution metrics}

For a fixed fitted model, the noiseless population prediction error is
\begin{equation}
  E_{\rm gen}(\that)
  =S_{\perp}+(\that-\tstar)^{\top}C(\that-\tstar).
  \label{eq:exact-gen-error}
\end{equation}
We use the noiseless-teacher coefficient of determination
\begin{equation}
  R^2_{\rm gen}=1-\frac{E_{\rm gen}}{S}.
  \label{eq:r2-gen}
\end{equation}
The slope of true response on predicted response over natural inputs is
\begin{equation}
  b_{\rm gen}
  =\frac{\Cov((\bstar)^{\top}\bm x,\that^{\top}A\bm x)}
         {\Var(\that^{\top}A\bm x)}
  =\frac{\bm h^{\top}\that}{\that^{\top}C\that}.
  \label{eq:slope-gen-exact}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Own-path accentuation metrics}

Gradient accentuation from a seed $\bm x_0$ follows
\begin{equation}
  \bm x(t)=\bm x_0+t\nabla_{\bm x}\widehat f(\bm x)
           =\bm x_0+tA^{\top}\that.
  \label{eq:feature-accent-path}
\end{equation}
The student and teacher changes along this path are, respectively,
\begin{align}
  \Delta\widehat f(t)&=t\that^{\top}G\that,\\
  \Delta f^{\star}(t)&=t\bm q^{\top}\that.
  \label{eq:path-gains}
\end{align}
Therefore the exact true-on-predicted slope along the path is
\begin{equation}
  b_{\rm acc}(\that)
  =\frac{\bm q^{\top}\that}{\that^{\top}G\that}.
  \label{eq:slope-acc-exact}
\end{equation}
Suppose the experimenter chooses the distribution of $t$ so that the
student's pathwise response variance equals the natural teacher variance $S$:
\begin{equation}
  \Var(t)=\frac{S}{(\that^{\top}G\that)^2}.
\end{equation}
The accentuation calibration error is then
\begin{equation}
  \frac{E_{\rm acc}}{S}
  =\left(1-b_{\rm acc}\right)^2
  =\left(1-\frac{\bm q^{\top}\that}
                   {\that^{\top}G\that}\right)^2.
  \label{eq:acc-error-exact}
\end{equation}
When the denominator of $R^2$ is the variance of the \emph{true} response on
the generated path, the exact pathwise statistic becomes
\begin{equation}
  R^2_{\rm acc}
  =1-\left(1-\frac{\that^{\top}G\that}
                        {\bm q^{\top}\that}\right)^2.
  \label{eq:r2-acc-exact}
\end{equation}
Unlike $E_{\rm acc}/S$, this statistic is singular when the true path gain
$\bm q^{\top}\that$ is close to zero.  Hence it can be extremely negative even
when other summary metrics appear bounded.

\section{Spectral feature maps and deterministic equivalents}
\label{sec:spectral-de}

We now specialize to the setting implemented in the accompanying numerical
experiments.  Write the input eigendecomposition as
\begin{equation}
  \Sigma=U\,\mathrm{diag}(s_1,\ldots,s_d)U^{\top},
  \qquad b_j=\bm u_j^{\top}\bstar.
\end{equation}
For an index set $\mathcal J$, let the feature map retain and rescale PCs,
\begin{equation}
  z_j=a_j\bm u_j^{\top}\bm x,\qquad j\in\mathcal J.
\end{equation}
In the common eigenbasis of prediction and control, define
\begin{equation}
  c_j=a_j^2s_j,\qquad
  g_j=a_j^2,\qquad
  \theta_j^{\star}=\frac{b_j}{a_j}.
  \label{eq:spectral-feature-quantities}
\end{equation}
Then $C=\mathrm{diag}(c_j)$, $G=\mathrm{diag}(g_j)$, and
$q_j=g_j\theta_j^{\star}$.  The omitted signal and effective noise are
\begin{equation}
  S_{\perp}=\sum_{j\notin\mathcal J}s_jb_j^2,qquad
  \sigma_{\rm eff}^2=\sigma^2+S_{\perp}.
\end{equation}

We take $p,n\to\infty$ with $p/n\to\gamma$.  The effective ridge parameter
$\kappa=\kappa(\lambda)$ solves the Silverstein fixed-point equation
\begin{equation}
  \kappa-\lambda
  =\frac{\kappa}{n}\sum_{j\in\mathcal J}
       \frac{c_j}{c_j+\kappa}.
  \label{eq:kappa-fixed-point}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the right side is
$\gamma\kappa\int c/(c+\kappa)\,d\nu(c)$ for the empirical feature spectrum
$\nu$.

Define
\begin{align}
  h_j&=\frac{c_j}{c_j+\kappa},\\
  \mathrm{df}_2(\kappa)&=\sum_j\frac{c_j^2}{(c_j+\kappa)^2},\\
  C_{\rm sig}(\kappa)&=
       \sum_j\frac{c_j(\theta_j^{\star})^2}{(c_j+\kappa)^2},\\
  v_0(\kappa)&=
       \frac{\kappa^2C_{\rm sig}(\kappa)+\sigma_{\rm eff}^2}
            {n-\mathrm{df}_2(\kappa)}.
  \label{eq:de-global-quantities}
\end{align}
The leading componentwise first and second moments of the fitted feature
weight are
\begin{align}
  m_j&\equiv\E[\widehat\theta_j]
       \simeq h_j\theta_j^{\star},\\
  v_j&\equiv\Var(\widehat\theta_j)
       \simeq v_0\frac{c_j}{(c_j+\kappa)^2},\\
  \E[\widehat\theta_j^2]&\simeq m_j^2+v_j.
  \label{eq:de-component-moments}
\end{align}
Thus the per-feature coefficient error is
\begin{equation}
  e_j\equiv\E[(\widehat\theta_j-\theta_j^{\star})^2]
  \simeq (1-h_j)^2(\theta_j^{\star})^2+v_j.
  \label{eq:de-per-feature-error}
\end{equation}
The first term is ridge shrinkage bias; the second combines finite-sample
signal and response-noise fluctuations.

\subsection{DE generalization metrics}

Aggregating the componentwise moments gives
\begin{align}
  E_{\rm gen}^{\rm DE}
    &=S_{\perp}+\sum_j c_je_j,
    \label{eq:de-gen-error}\\
  R_{\rm gen}^{2,\rm DE}
    &=1-\frac{E_{\rm gen}^{\rm DE}}{S},\\
  N_{\rm gen}^{\rm DE}
    &=\sum_j c_jm_j\theta_j^{\star},\\
  D_{\rm gen}^{\rm DE}
    &=\sum_j c_j(m_j^2+v_j),\\
  b_{\rm gen}^{\rm DE}
    &=\frac{N_{\rm gen}^{\rm DE}}{D_{\rm gen}^{\rm DE}}.
  \label{eq:de-gen-slope}
\end{align}

\subsection{DE pixel-accentuation metrics}

The control geometry changes only the aggregation weights:
\begin{align}
  N_{\rm acc}^{\rm DE}
    &=\sum_j g_jm_j\theta_j^{\star},\\
  D_{\rm acc}^{\rm DE}
    &=\sum_j g_j(m_j^2+v_j),\\
  b_{\rm acc}^{\rm DE}
    &=\frac{N_{\rm acc}^{\rm DE}}{D_{\rm acc}^{\rm DE}},
    \label{eq:de-acc-slope}\\
  \frac{E_{\rm acc}^{\rm DE}}{S}
    &=\left(1-b_{\rm acc}^{\rm DE}\right)^2,\\
  R_{\rm acc}^{2,\rm DE}
    &=1-\left(1-\frac{D_{\rm acc}^{\rm DE}}
                            {N_{\rm acc}^{\rm DE}}\right)^2.
    \label{eq:de-acc-r2}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:de-gen-error}--\eqref{eq:de-acc-r2} are the main
feature-space extension.  Natural generalization weights mode $j$ by $c_j$;
pixel accentuation weights it by $g_j$.  The two agree only in special cases.

The DE above substitutes ratios of deterministic first and second moments for
the nonlinear per-fit ratios.  It predicts a typical high-dimensional fit but
is not, in general, the exact Monte Carlo mean of $R^2_{\rm acc}$.  This
distinction matters whenever $N_{\rm acc}$ can approach zero: rare fits then
dominate the empirical mean, while the median can remain close to the leading
DE.

\subsection{Input-space weight recovery}

The recovered pixel coefficient in retained mode $j$ is
$\widehat b_j=a_j\widehat\theta_j$.  Therefore
\begin{align}
  \E[\widehat b_j]&\simeq a_jm_j,\\
  \Var(\widehat b_j)&\simeq g_jv_j,\\
  \E\lVert\bhat-\bstar\rVert^2
    &\simeq \sum_{j\in\mathcal J}g_je_j
          +\sum_{j\notin\mathcal J}b_j^2.
  \label{eq:de-pixel-weight-error}
\end{align}
This formula makes the high-frequency mechanism explicit.  A low-variance
direction can make a negligible contribution $s_j e_j$ to prediction error
but a large contribution $g_je_j$ to the back-propagated pixel gradient.
The DE predicts the mean and variance envelope across modes; it does not
predict the signs or texture of one particular random fitted realization.

\section{Cross-validated ridge selection from the DE}

Many software packages parameterize ridge as
\begin{equation}
  \lVert\bm y-Z\bm\theta\rVert^2+\alpha\lVert\bm\theta\rVert^2,
\end{equation}
so that $\lambda=\alpha/n$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:feature-ridge}.  For analytic
leave-one-out cross-validation, a DE approximation follows the actual
training-size change:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item set $n_{\rm tr}=n-1$;
  \item for every candidate $\alpha$, solve
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:kappa-fixed-point} with
        $\lambda=\alpha/n_{\rm tr}$ and aspect ratio $p/n_{\rm tr}$;
  \item compute $E_{\rm gen}^{\rm DE}(\alpha;n_{\rm tr})$ using
        $\sigma_{\rm eff}^2$;
  \item select
        $\alpha_{\rm DE-CV}=\arg\min_{\alpha}E_{\rm gen}^{\rm DE}$;
  \item refit the final $n$-sample DE with
        $\lambda=\alpha_{\rm DE-CV}/n$.
\end{enumerate}
For $K$-fold CV, replace $n-1$ by the fold training size.  A dense log-spaced
$\alpha$ grid is important: a coarse grid creates artificial jumps in the
selected regularization and therefore jagged downstream control metrics.
The DE selects ridge for natural predictive risk, just as ordinary CV does;
it does not secretly optimize accentuation.

\section{PCA, whitening, and top-PC truncation}

The spectral formulas expose the behavior of the three feature spaces used in
the FFHQ disk-teacher experiment.

\label{tab:feature-cases}

\paragraph{Full PCA.}
An orthogonal change of basis leaves ridge and the pixel-gradient norm
unchanged.  It is the pixel-space baseline written in interpretable spectral
coordinates.

\paragraph{Full whitening.}
Regression sees an isotropic covariance, $c_j=1$, but back-propagation weights
each mode by $g_j=1/s_j$.  Low-variance, high-frequency PCs can therefore
dominate $D_{\rm acc}$ even though they do not dominate natural prediction.
The feature representation may look well conditioned while pixel control is
ill conditioned.

\paragraph{Top-PC truncation.}
Discarding low-variance PCs introduces irreducible natural error
$S_{\perp}$, but completely prevents those directions from entering the
pixel-gradient path.  When the teacher signal is concentrated in the leading
PCs, a modest predictive cost can buy a large control benefit.  The optimal
cutoff depends on noise: higher response noise generally favors a smaller,
more signal-focused feature space.

\section{Peer review across models and feature spaces}

Let an evaluator have pixel weight
$\widehat{\bm w}_e=A_e^{\top}\widehat{\bm\theta}_e$ and a generator have
$\widehat{\bm w}_g=A_g^{\top}\widehat{\bm\theta}_g$.  Along the generator's
path $\bm x(t)=\bm x_0+t\widehat{\bm w}_g$, the teacher and evaluator gains
are
\begin{equation}
  T_g=(\bstar)^{\top}\widehat{\bm w}_g,\qquad
  D_{e\leftarrow g}=\widehat{\bm w}_e^{\top}\widehat{\bm w}_g
  =\widehat{\bm\theta}_e^{\top}A_eA_g^{\top}
       \widehat{\bm\theta}_g.
\end{equation}
If the generator scales its path to its own natural-response target range,
the evaluator's normalized peer error, slope, and $R^2$ are
\begin{align}
  \frac{E_{e\leftarrow g}}{S}
    &=\left(\frac{D_{e\leftarrow g}-T_g}
                    {\widehat{\bm w}_g^{\top}\widehat{\bm w}_g}\right)^2,\\
  b_{e\leftarrow g}&=\frac{T_g}{D_{e\leftarrow g}},\\
  R^2_{e\leftarrow g}
    &=1-\left(1-\frac{D_{e\leftarrow g}}{T_g}\right)^2.
  \label{eq:peer-r2-feature}
\end{align}
For two independently trained models, a leading peer DE can replace each
weight by its deterministic mean in $D_{e\leftarrow g}$ and $T_g$.  Models
trained on the same or overlapping samples require a joint two-resolvent DE
to retain their correlated fluctuations.  Equation~\eqref{eq:peer-r2-feature}
also clarifies the observed asymmetry: a generator can move in directions
that remain legible to a good evaluator, while the reverse path can have
nearly zero teacher gain.

\section{Interpretation and limitations}

The main conclusions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Good natural-image prediction does not imply good pixel-space
        control.  Prediction uses $C$; control uses $G$.
  \item Low-variance modes are cheap under natural risk but can be expensive
        under pixel-gradient accentuation, especially after whitening.
  \item Top-PC truncation can reverse the generalization--accentuation gap by
        removing directions that contribute little teacher signal but large
        control variance.
  \item The feature-space DE predicts mean per-mode bias and variance,
        aggregate natural risk, typical control calibration, and DE-CV ridge
        selection.  It does not specify the visual pattern of a single random
        fit.
  \item $R^2_{\rm acc}$ and peer $R^2$ are nonlinear ratios.  Near-zero true
        path gain creates heavy tails, so empirical medians/IQRs and
        means/standard errors should both be reported.
\end{enumerate}

The present closed-form feature DE assumes a linear feature map aligned with
the input covariance eigenbasis, so that $C$ and $G$ commute.  The exact
finite-sample formulas in Sections 1--2 hold for arbitrary linear $A$.
Extending the DE to noncommuting $C$ and $G$, nonlinear learned features, and
joint peer-review fluctuations requires operator-valued or bilinear-resolvent
deterministic equivalents.
